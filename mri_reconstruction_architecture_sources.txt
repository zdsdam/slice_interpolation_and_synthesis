Sources and Architecture Traceability for the SynthSR-Inspired MRI Reconstruction MVP

The architecture diagram and project structure were based on a combination of published MRI super-resolution methods, the SynthSR methodology, standard 3D U-Net design, and 3D Slicer development material. A few parts—mainly the exact file/module organization—were software-engineering design choices rather than something copied directly from a paper.

1. Iglesias et al. — SynthSR

Title:
“Joint super-resolution and synthesis of 1 mm isotropic MP-RAGE volumes from clinical MRI exams with scans of different orientation, resolution and contrast”

Role in the project:
This is the primary source behind the overall research pipeline. SynthSR takes low-resolution or spaced-slice MRI and learns to produce an isotropic high-resolution output. A major methodological idea is synthetic data generation rather than requiring real paired low-resolution/high-resolution acquisitions.

This informed:

HR / synthetic source
       ↓
Synthetic degradation
       ↓
Low-resolution / spaced-slice MRI
       ↓
CNN reconstruction
       ↓
Isotropic HR MRI

It is also the main justification for making the synthetic sparse-slice simulator a core research component, even if real sparse scans later become available.

2. Du et al. — Residual CNN for anisotropic MRI super-resolution

Title:
“Super-resolution reconstruction of single anisotropic 3D MR images using residual convolutional neural network”

Role in the project:
This work directly targets reconstruction of high-resolution isotropic MRI from anisotropic MRI. It uses residual learning and CNN-based reconstruction to recover high-frequency information.

This informed:

Interpolated sparse / anisotropic MRI
       ↓
CNN predicts missing information
       ↓
Residual / skip connection
       ↓
High-resolution reconstruction

It is the main source behind the residual formulation:

reconstruction = interpolated input + predicted residual

It also informed the use of interpolation as a baseline and evaluation with metrics such as PSNR and SSIM.

3. Çiçek et al. — 3D U-Net

Title:
“3D U-Net: Learning Dense Volumetric Segmentation from Sparse Annotation”

Role in the project:
Although this is a volumetric segmentation paper rather than a super-resolution paper, it provides the architectural basis for a 3D U-Net using:

- 3D convolutions
- encoder/downsampling path
- bottleneck
- decoder/upsampling path
- skip connections between matching resolutions

This informed the proposed model:

3D U-Net

Encoder
↓
Bottleneck
↓
Decoder

with encoder-to-decoder skip connections.

4. Broader deep-learning MRI super-resolution literature

Role in the project:
The broader literature supports framing the task as:

Low-resolution / anisotropic MRI
       ↓
Deep reconstruction model
       ↓
High-resolution / isotropic MRI

It also supports:
- interpolation baselines
- residual learning
- quantitative comparison against ground truth
- metrics such as PSNR and SSIM

5. Queen’s 3D Slicer Programming Tutorial

Role in the project:
The tutorial informed the decision to use 3D Slicer mainly as the visualization and validation endpoint instead of placing the entire machine-learning pipeline inside Slicer.

Relevant concepts from the tutorial include:
- scripted Python modules for prototyping
- separation of GUI and processing logic
- MRML volume nodes
- NumPy access to volumetric data
- loading and manipulating medical-image volumes

This supports the project endpoint:

Reconstructed volume
       ↓
.nii.gz
       ↓
3D Slicer
       ↓
Visual inspection and comparison

6. Slicer Developer Tutorial by Pujol and Pieper

Role in the project:
This tutorial similarly describes Python scripted modules as suitable for fast prototyping and workflow development and shows that Slicer provides direct access to medical-image volumes and NumPy arrays.

This supports:
- exporting reconstructed volumes as NIfTI
- opening them in 3D Slicer
- eventual integration into a Slicer module if needed

Software-engineering choices that were not taken directly from the papers

The following repository structure was an engineering recommendation:

project/
│
├── data/
│
├── src/
│   ├── data_loader.py
│   ├── preprocessing.py
│   ├── sparse_simulator.py
│   ├── interpolation.py
│   │
│   ├── models/
│   │   ├── blocks.py
│   │   └── unet3d.py
│   │
│   ├── losses.py
│   ├── metrics.py
│   ├── train.py
│   ├── evaluate.py
│   ├── inference.py
│   └── export_nifti.py
│
├── checkpoints/
├── outputs/
└── requirements.txt

No paper requires this exact structure. It was chosen because each file corresponds to a distinct stage of the research pipeline and can be tested independently.

The following were also practical MVP choices rather than exact settings taken from SynthSR:

- 64×64×64 training patches
- Adam optimizer
- learning rate around 1e-4
- 16 → 32 → 64 → 128 feature channels
- L1 loss as the first loss function
- sliding-window inference
